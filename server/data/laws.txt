1. Constitutional Foundation
1. Article 300A – Right to Property
Property is a constitutional and human right.
No person shall be deprived of their property except by authority of law.
2. Central / Pan-India Laws
Property Ownership & Transfer
The Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (TPA)
Governs sale, gift, mortgage, lease, exchange, and transfer of immovable property.
The Registration Act, 1908
Mandates registration of sale deeds, gift deeds, mortgages, and leases exceeding 11 months.
The Indian Stamp Act, 1899
Governs stamp duty on legal documents.
The Specific Relief Act, 1963
Allows enforcement of property sale agreements and specific performance.
The Limitation Act, 1963
Prescribes time limits for filing property-related lawsuits.
Real Estate Regulation
The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (RERA)
Protects homebuyers and regulates builders and developers.
National Building Code of India (NBC)
Sets standards for structural safety, fire safety, accessibility, and construction practices.
Contracts & Agreements
The Indian Contract Act, 1872
Governs agreements between buyers, sellers, builders, contractors, suppliers, and engineers.
The Indian Easements Act, 1882
Governs Leave & License Agreements and easement rights.
Consumer Protection
The Consumer Protection Act, 2019
Allows buyers to file complaints against builders and developers for deficiency in service.
Illegal Property Transactions
The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988
Prohibits purchasing property in another person's name to conceal ownership.
Land Acquisition
The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013
Governs government acquisition of private land.
Builder Insolvency
The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016
Gives homebuyers rights when developers become insolvent.
Environmental Laws
The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986
Regulates environmental clearances for construction projects.
The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980
Restricts construction on forest land.
The Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958
Regulates construction near protected monuments.
Banking & Finance
The SARFAESI Act, 2002
Governs bank recovery of secured assets and mortgage enforcement.
The Income Tax Act, 1961 (Property Provisions)
Covers capital gains, TDS on property purchase, and tax benefits on housing loans.
The Goods and Services Tax (GST) Act, 2017
Governs GST applicable to under-construction properties and builder services.
Succession & Family Property
The Hindu Succession Act, 1956
Governs inheritance among Hindus.
The Indian Succession Act, 1925
Governs wills and succession for applicable communities.
The Partition Act, 1893
Governs division of jointly owned property.
Digital & Electronic Records
The Information Technology Act, 2000
Recognizes electronic records, digital signatures, and online agreements.
The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023
Governs admissibility of electronic records and digital evidence.

6. Key Statutory Provisions — Detailed Reference
Transfer of Property Act 1882, Section 54: A "sale" of immovable property is defined as a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price; for tangible immovable property valued at Rs. 100 or more, the transfer must be made only by a registered instrument, and a mere unregistered agreement to sell does not by itself transfer ownership or create any interest in the property.
Transfer of Property Act 1882, Section 55(1)(a): Before completing a sale, the seller has a legal duty to disclose to the buyer any material defect in the property or in the seller's own title that the seller is aware of and the buyer is not, and which the buyer could not discover with ordinary care; deliberately concealing such a defect can amount to fraud, and this duty can only be excluded by an express contrary term in the agreement, such as a genuine "as is where is" clause.
Transfer of Property Act 1882, Section 55(1)(b) and (c): The seller must produce all title documents in their possession for the buyer's inspection on request, and must truthfully answer all relevant questions the buyer raises about the property or the title, so the buyer can properly assess ownership and encumbrances before paying.
Transfer of Property Act 1882, Section 55(2): Every sale carries an implied statutory warranty that the seller actually has the right to transfer the interest they are professing to sell; if the seller's title later turns out to be defective, this implied warranty gives the buyer a legal basis to claim compensation even if the sale agreement is silent on the point.
Transfer of Property Act 1882, Section 58: A mortgage is the transfer of an interest in immovable property to secure repayment of a loan or performance of an obligation, and the Act recognizes several types including simple mortgage, mortgage by conditional sale, usufructuary mortgage, English mortgage, and mortgage by deposit of title deeds (equitable mortgage); a property that is already mortgaged carries an encumbrance that must be disclosed and cleared before a clean sale can proceed.
Indian Registration Act 1908, Section 17: Registration is compulsory for instruments of gift of immovable property, non-testamentary documents that create, declare, assign, limit, or extinguish any right, title, or interest worth Rs. 100 or more in immovable property, and leases of immovable property for a term exceeding one year; a plain unregistered "agreement to sell" for a high-value property is generally not, by itself, one of these compulsorily registrable documents, but any document that actually purports to convey or create ownership rights above this threshold must be registered.
Indian Registration Act 1908, Section 49: A document that was legally required to be registered but was not will not, in general, be treated as affecting the immovable property it describes, will not confer any right, title, or interest in that property, and cannot be used as evidence of the transaction in court — with a narrow exception allowing it as evidence in a suit for specific performance or as proof of a collateral transaction; this is why an unregistered sale deed or conveyance is a serious red flag in a document review.
Indian Contract Act 1872, Section 73: When a contract is breached, the party who suffers is entitled to compensation for the loss that naturally arose from the breach in the ordinary course of things, or which both parties knew at the time of contracting was likely to result from a breach; this is the baseline right to damages that applies even without any specific penalty clause in the agreement.
Indian Contract Act 1872, Section 74: Where a contract names a specific sum as a penalty for breach, or contains any other stipulation "by way of penalty" (such as a builder or buyer forfeiting a token deposit versus the entire booking amount), the aggrieved party can only recover reasonable compensation not exceeding that named amount — courts do not automatically enforce the full penalty figure, and in Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Das the Supreme Court held that Section 74 requires courts to award only what is reasonable, striking down excessive forfeiture clauses even when the contract's wording appears to allow the full amount.
Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 (RERA), Section 18: If a registered developer fails to hand over possession by the date committed in the agreement for sale, or fails to complete the project as per the sanctioned plans and specifications, the buyer has two options: withdraw from the project and receive a full refund of all amounts paid along with interest, or continue with the project and receive interest for every month of delay until possession is actually given. The Supreme Court, in Newtech Promoters and Developers v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2021), described this Section 18 right as "unconditional and absolute," meaning contract clauses that try to cap or condition this compensation at a token figure do not bind the buyer.
Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 (RERA), escrow requirement: Promoters of registered projects must deposit at least 70% of the amounts realized from buyers for a project into a separate escrow account, to be used only for the construction and land cost of that specific project; this is intended to stop developers from diverting buyer funds to other projects and leaving a project unfinished.
Hindu Succession Act 1956, Section 8: When a Hindu male dies intestate (without a will), his property first devolves upon the Class I heirs specified in the Schedule to the Act, which includes his widow, sons, daughters, and mother, among others, who all take the property simultaneously and in equal shares in that class; only in the absence of any Class I heir does the property pass to Class II heirs or other categories.
Hindu Succession Act 1956, Section 6 (as amended in 2005): Daughters of a coparcener in a Hindu Mitakshara joint family are, by birth, coparceners in their own right in the same manner as sons, and have the same rights and liabilities in the coparcenary property as a son; this retrospectively gave daughters equal inheritance rights in ancestral property, which is a common point of dispute in older family-owned properties being sold, since a sale without the consent of all rightful coparceners, including daughters, can later be challenged.
Indian Stamp Act 1899, Section 35: An instrument that is not duly stamped as required by law is generally not admissible in evidence in any court proceeding, and cannot be acted upon, registered, or authenticated by any public officer, until the deficient stamp duty (and often a penalty) is paid; this means underpaying stamp duty to save money at the time of purchase can make the sale deed itself unusable as legal evidence later, undermining the very title it was meant to prove.
Maharashtra Ownership Flats Act (MOFA) 1963: A builder or promoter selling flats in Maharashtra is legally required to enter into a written, registered agreement with each buyer, maintain a separate account for booking advances, and — once the flats are sold and the building is complete — take the necessary steps to form a cooperative housing society or condominium and convey (transfer) title of the land and building to that society within the time prescribed by the rules; a long-pending "society formation" or "conveyance" after possession is a well-known compliance gap builders in Maharashtra sometimes leave unresolved.
Specific Relief Act 1963: Where a party refuses to perform their obligations under a valid property sale agreement, the aggrieved party may seek a court order for "specific performance" — compelling the actual completion of the sale as agreed, rather than only monetary damages — which is often the more meaningful remedy for a buyer when the specific property, and not just its market value, is what they were relying on.
Consumer Protection Act 2019: A home buyer qualifies as a "consumer," and can file a complaint against a builder or developer before the Consumer Commission for "deficiency in service," such as poor construction quality, non-delivery of promised amenities, or unfair contract terms, as an option that can run in parallel to a RERA complaint, though RERA is generally the more specialized forum for project-specific issues like delayed possession.
Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act 1988 (as amended 2016): It is illegal to purchase property in the name of another person (a "benami" transaction) while the real purchaser provides the funds and enjoys the benefits, with limited exceptions such as property held for a spouse or child, or by a person in a fiduciary capacity such as a trustee; violations can lead to confiscation of the property by the government and criminal prosecution of both the person providing the funds and the person whose name is used.
